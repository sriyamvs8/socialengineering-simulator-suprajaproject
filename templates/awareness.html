What Is Phishing?

Phishing is a form of social engineering where attackers impersonate a trusted person, brand, or organization to trick someone into revealing sensitive information — passwords, card numbers, OTPs — or into installing malware. Unlike technical hacking, phishing exploits human trust and urgency rather than software flaws. It typically arrives through email, text messages, phone calls, or fake websites that closely mimic the real thing.

Common Types of Phishing

Email Phishing

Mass emails impersonating banks, delivery services, or platforms, urging urgent action on a fake link.

Spear Phishing

Highly targeted messages aimed at one specific person, often using personal details to appear credible.

Whaling

Spear phishing aimed at senior executives, often to authorize large wire transfers or leak confidential data.

Smishing

Phishing delivered via SMS text messages, often claiming a package delivery issue or account alert.

Vishing

Voice-call phishing where attackers impersonate support staff, banks, or officials over the phone.

Clone Phishing

A legitimate email that was previously delivered is copied and resent with malicious links swapped in.

Real-World Case Studies

Target Corporation — 2013

Attackers first breached a third-party HVAC vendor using a phishing email, then used the vendor's stolen network credentials to move into Target's payment systems. The result was one of the largest retail breaches on record, exposing the payment card details of roughly 40 million customers. It remains a textbook example of how phishing a smaller, less-secure supplier can open the door to a much larger organization.

Google & Facebook Invoice Scam — 2013–2015

A Lithuanian man ran a business-email-compromise scheme by impersonating a hardware supplier that both companies genuinely worked with. He sent convincing fake invoices and forged corporate documents, tricking finance staff into wiring more than 100 million dollars combined to accounts he controlled before the scheme was uncovered.

Ubiquiti Networks — 2015

Attackers impersonated senior executives and outside legal counsel through spoofed email domains, instructing the finance department to complete confidential, time-sensitive wire transfers. The company transferred over 46 million dollars before recognizing the fraud, later recovering only a portion of the funds.

Twitter Verified-Account Breach — 2020

Attackers used phone-based spear phishing (vishing) to trick Twitter employees into revealing internal credentials. This gave them access to internal admin tools, which they used to take over high-profile verified accounts and post a cryptocurrency scam that collected payments from unsuspecting followers within minutes.

Red Flags To Watch For

  • Urgent or threatening language ("act now", "account will be suspended")
  • Sender address that looks close but not identical to the real domain
  • Generic greetings like "Dear Customer" instead of your actual name
  • Links that don't match the text shown when you hover over them
  • Requests for passwords, OTPs, or payment details over email or text
  • Unexpected attachments, especially .exe, .zip, or macro-enabled files
  • Poor grammar, spelling mistakes, or inconsistent formatting
  • Too-good-to-be-true offers or unexpected prize/refund notifications

What To Do If You Suspect Phishing

1. Don't Click

Avoid clicking links or opening attachments in suspicious messages.

2. Verify Separately

Contact the organization directly using a known number or website, not the one in the message.

3. Report It

Forward the message to your IT/security team or use your email provider's "Report Phishing" option.

4. Change Passwords

If you already entered credentials, change that password immediately and enable MFA.
